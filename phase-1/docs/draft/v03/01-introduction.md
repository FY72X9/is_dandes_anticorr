## 1. Introduction

Indonesia's *Dana Desa* (Village Fund) programme represents one of the largest intergovernmental fiscal transfers in the developing world, channelling approximately Rp 71 trillion annually to 75,259 villages across the archipelago since the enactment of Law No. 6 of 2014 on Villages [1]. The programme's fiscal scale, however, has severely outpaced the supervisory infrastructure needed to ensure its financial integrity. By 2024, Indonesian Corruption Watch (ICW) had catalogued 591 court verdicts involving village fund misappropriation, implicating 640 defendants and documenting Rp 598.13 billion in verified state financial losses [2]. Concurrently, the Komisi Pemberantasan Korupsi (KPK) identified 851 village fund corruption cases from 2015 onward, in which village heads (*Kepala Desa*) accounted for over 60% of perpetrators [3]. These empirical figures converge on a single diagnostic conclusion: the dominant institutional response to village fund corruption remains reactive—prosecution occurring two to five years after fund diversion—rather than proactive, data-driven detection operating within active disbursement cycles.

The technical infrastructure for proactive detection already exists in latent administrative databases. The *Sistem Keuangan Desa* (Siskeudes), maintained by BPKP and the Ministry of Home Affairs (*Kemendagri*), records village fund expenditure at the granular activity level across each disbursement stage: what type of output activity was planned, how much budget was allocated (*Pagu*), how much was realised per tranche (T1, T2, T3), and through what procurement mechanism (*Cara Pengadaan*). This granular absorption data constitutes an administrative signal archive—one that encodes, in latent statistical form, the expenditure patterns that distinguish legitimate governance from fraudulent diversion. Yet no systematic, automated screening mechanism currently operates at the district or provincial level to interrogate this archive before inspection cycles conclude and disbursement windows close.

Existing research on *Dana Desa* fraud concentrates overwhelmingly on retrospective forensic analysis [4], qualitative governance accountability frameworks [5], and traditional fraud triangle diagnostics [6]. Where machine learning enters the literature, it applies predominantly supervised classification—a paradigm that requires pre-existing fraud labels unavailable in real-time administrative monitoring contexts [13, 14]. The present study addresses this gap directly through a **Design Science Research (DSR)** approach [5, 10]: it designs, constructs, and evaluates a multi-paradigm unsupervised learning artifact (Protocol 2) that detects expenditure anomalies across a longitudinal panel of 96,778 activity-level expenditure records (1,363 villages, 3 fiscal years 2023–2025) in Jambi Province, without presupposing any prior fraud labels.

**Jambi Province provides a structurally significant research site.** The `jaga.id` civil-society transparency platform, operated by KPK, recorded only 11 complaint submissions attributed to Jambi Province—1.4% of 761 national reports—while provinces of comparable administrative scale registered substantially higher reporting volumes [26]. Low voluntary public reporting does not signal low corruption exposure; rather, it signals a low-monitoring environment. Alfada's panel GMM analysis across 19 Indonesian provinces confirms that regions combining high intergovernmental transfer dependence with weak accountability structures exhibit systematically elevated corruption incidence—the structural profile directly applicable to Jambi [27]. Furthermore, district-level *Aparat Pengawasan Intern Pemerintah* (APIP) inspectorates face severe operational capacity constraints: Srirejeki and Faturokhman [12] show that kabupaten APIP offices lack staffing to manually screen thousands of individual village activity records per disbursement cycle, producing a measurable shortfall between the statutory monitoring mandate under Government Regulation No. 60/2008 and actual physical inspection coverage.

Contemporaneous court verdicts in Jambi Province—collectively documenting Rp 2.301 billion in verified state losses across Kerinci, Muaro Jambi, and Tanjung Jabung Timur jurisdictions [28, 29, 30, 31]—confirm that detection lags range from two to five years. All four judicial cases involved financial irregularities that generated quantifiable signals in Siskeudes absorption records: fictitious construction outputs, zero-realisation procurement activities, and uncompetitive Swakelola budget execution—precisely the patterns targeted by the feature engineering and Dual-Path consensus architecture in this study.

This study makes three main contributions:
1. **Methodological Contribution (DSR Artifact)**: It constructs an unsupervised Dual-Path Consensus Machine Learning framework combining local density estimation (LOF) and global anomaly convergence (IF $\cap$ RDA) with an Operational Policy Mapping Layer, resolving the mutual cancellation limitation of traditional majority voting.
2. **Theoretical Contribution**: It operationalises Agency Theory, the Fraud Diamond, and Principal-Agent information asymmetry within administrative Siskeudes records, bridging Information Systems (IS) governance literature and computational fraud detection.
3. **Practical Contribution**: It provides an ex-ante evaluation framework that reduces APIP inspection search space by 92.6% at the activity level and prioritises 702 persistent high-risk villages (Tier 1), generating instance-level Explainable AI (XAI) audit checklists for field inspectors.

This study addresses three core Research Questions (RQs):
- **RQ1**: Which feature constructs derived from Siskeudes expenditure absorption data serve as the most discriminating signals of expenditure anomaly?
- **RQ2**: Which algorithmic paradigm—Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, or Reconstruction Dense Autoencoder—and ensemble gating mechanism demonstrates superior anomaly discrimination on longitudinal village fund data?
- **RQ3**: How do algorithmically identified anomalies map to established corruption typologies, and how can XAI loss decomposition translate mathematical outliers into actionable APIP inspection checklists?

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on corruption typologies, fraud theory, anomaly detection paradigms, and IS success frameworks. Section 3 details the DSR methodology, dataset cleaning, regional baseline centering, feature engineering, mathematical algorithm specifications, and Dual-Path ensemble gate. Section 4 presents comprehensive empirical results and benchmark evaluations. Section 5 interprets findings through DSR evaluation principles, 5-Whys causal chains, and practical APIP audit protocols. Section 6 concludes with key contributions and future research directions.
